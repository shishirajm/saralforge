A website builder is a hosted tool with templates that you edit yourself. A custom website is built to your requirements, sometimes on top of a content management system and sometimes from scratch. Neither is better in general. The right choice depends on what the site must do and who will look after it.

When a builder is usually enough

For many small businesses, a builder does the job and saves time. It tends to suit you if most of these are true:

  • The site mainly explains what you do, shows examples and lets people contact you.
  • You or your staff want to edit text and photos without asking anyone.
  • You need a simple contact form and perhaps a standard booking or payment widget.
  • You are happy to work within the templates and features the platform offers.
  • You need to be live quickly and can adjust the design later.

When requirements justify custom work

Custom work is worth considering when the site has to do something that a template does not do well, for example:

  • Bookings or quotes that follow rules specific to your business, such as travel time between jobs or approval steps.
  • Information that must flow to or from other systems you use, such as a customer or job system.
  • Logins where different people see different things.
  • A structure that does not fit the platform, such as a large catalogue with unusual relationships.
  • Specific requirements for accessibility, speed or privacy that you cannot meet by configuring the builder.

Even then, the answer may be a builder site with one custom piece added, not a full custom build.

Whichever you choose, accessibility, speed and clear content are your responsibility. Builders can help or get in the way, so test the actual result on a phone, with a keyboard and with your own eyes.

Questions about ownership and upkeep

These matter more than the first-year cost. Ask them of any builder and any developer.

  • Is the domain name registered in your business's name?
  • Can you export your pages, images and customer data in a usable form?
  • What happens to the site if you stop paying, or if the provider changes its terms?
  • Who applies updates and security fixes, and how often?
  • Who can change things if the person who built it is unavailable?

Hypothetical example

A fictional landscaping business needs a services page, a gallery and a quote request form. A builder fits well: the owner can add new project photos herself. Later, she wants quote requests to appear in her job-tracking tool automatically. She keeps the builder site and talks to a developer about a small connection, rather than rebuilding the whole website.

What you can do before spending anything

  1. List what the site must do, in order of importance. Separate "must" from "nice".
  2. Try two builders with their free trials or demos, using your real content.
  3. Check what each lets you export.
  4. Use the website checklist to see what you actually need on the site.

Before you commit to a platform

Ask for advice when your list includes connections to other systems, logins with permissions, or rules that no template mentions. Also ask if you are about to commit to a platform and cannot tell whether you could leave it later. The guide to cost factors explains what pushes a quote up or down.
