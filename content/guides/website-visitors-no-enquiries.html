When a website gets visitors but no enquiries, the instinct is to redesign it. Sometimes that helps. More often, one specific thing is stopping people, and you can find it with an hour of checking and no special tools.

First, decide which kind of problem you have

There are two different problems that look the same from the outside. A traffic problem means the wrong people are arriving, or too few of them. A conversion problem means the right people arrive but do not take the next step. Conversion simply means a visitor doing the thing you want, such as sending an enquiry or booking a call.

Fixing the wrong one wastes money. More traffic to a page nobody can act on changes nothing, and a better page does little if the people arriving never wanted what you sell.

A self-audit, in order

Work through these in sequence. The early checks are quick and catch the most embarrassing problems first.

  1. Send yourself an enquiry. Use the form or number exactly as a customer would, from a different device. Does it arrive? How quickly? Is the confirmation message true? A form that looks fine but loses messages is the cheapest problem to fix and the most expensive to ignore. The guide to enquiry forms has a delivery-testing checklist.
  2. Use the site on a phone. Can you read the text without zooming, tap the buttons, and complete the form with a thumb? Check on a slow connection too.
  3. Run a five-second test. Show the homepage to someone who does not know your business. Ask: what does this business do, who is it for, and what would you do next? If they hesitate, your offer is unclear.
  4. Check who is actually arriving. If you have analytics, look at where visitors come from. If you do not, Google Search Console is free and shows the searches that lead people to your pages. Ask whether those people would plausibly want what you offer.
  5. Look for proof. Do you show real examples of your work, who you have helped, how you work, or what a customer can expect? Strangers need a reason to believe you. Only use proof you can stand behind. Do not invent reviews or numbers.
  6. Count the steps to contact you. Is there one obvious next step on each page? Is a phone number or email visible without hunting? Does the form ask for more than you need?
  7. Check what happens after an enquiry. If replies take days, or nobody owns the inbox, the website is not the problem.

Why a redesign is not automatically the answer

A redesign changes how the site looks and often how it is organised. It does not fix a form that drops messages, an offer that is hard to understand, or an inbox nobody checks. It can also introduce new problems, such as lost search visibility if page addresses change without redirects.

A redesign is worth considering when the structure itself works against your visitors: key information is buried, the site cannot be edited, or it does not work well on phones. Even then, the audit above tells you what the new design must fix.

Hypothetical example

A fictional electrician has a site that shows photos of past jobs but never says which suburbs are served or whether quotes are free. The contact form needs eight fields, and the confirmation message says "Thanks!" even when the message fails to send. The fixes are small: add a service area and a line about quotes, cut the form to three fields, and repair the delivery. None of them needs a new design.

What you can do this week without hiring anyone

  • Send three test enquiries from different devices and record when each arrives.
  • Rewrite the first screen of your homepage so it states what you do, for whom, and the next step.
  • Remove form fields you do not need to reply.
  • Add a real example of your work or a plain description of how you work.
  • Put one primary contact action on each page.
  • Decide who checks the inbox and by when.

When to ask for a second pair of eyes

Some problems are hard to see from the outside. Consider asking for help when:

  • Test enquiries sometimes arrive and sometimes do not. Intermittent faults usually involve email delivery settings or spam filtering.
  • You cannot tell where visitors come from, or the numbers do not make sense.
  • The fix needs your website to talk to a booking or customer system.
  • You have fixed the basics and still see no change, and the cause needs measurement rather than guesswork.

A short, focused review can tell you whether you have a traffic problem, a conversion problem or a process problem. If you decide to go further, a clear project brief makes any conversation faster and cheaper.
