An enquiry form has one job: let a stranger tell you what they need so you can reply well. Forms often fail in one of two ways. They ask for too much and put people off, or they look fine and quietly lose the message.

What to ask for

Ask for what you need to reply usefully, and no more. You can ask for detail in the reply once a conversation has started.

Typical enquiry form fields
FieldInclude?Why
NameYesSo you know how to address the reply.
Email or phoneYes, at least oneYou need a way to reply. Consider letting people choose.
What do you need?YesThis is the enquiry. Give a hint of what helps, such as location and timing.
Business nameOptionalUseful if you serve businesses. Mark it as optional.
BudgetOptional, if at allMany people do not know yet. "Not sure" must be a valid answer.
Address, date of birth, other detailsNo, unless the job needs itCollect the minimum. Extra personal details increase your responsibility without helping the reply.

Make it work for real people

  • Label every field with visible text that stays visible while typing. Do not rely on a faint placeholder as the only label.
  • Say what is required and what is optional.
  • Use the right keyboard on phones. An email field should bring up the email keyboard, and a phone field the number pad.
  • Explain errors in words next to the field, and keep what the person has already typed.
  • Make it usable by keyboard alone, with a visible focus indicator.
  • Make the button say what happens, such as "Send enquiry".

The W3C's forms guidance is a good plain reference. It recommends labelling each control, giving instructions where they help, validating input with useful messages and grouping related fields.

Spam without making people suffer

Forms attract automated spam. A hidden field that real people never fill in, combined with checks on the receiving side, can reduce it without asking visitors to solve puzzles. Be cautious with tools that add friction for everyone to stop a few bots.

The part most people skip: where does the message go?

Three very different things can sit behind a form's button.

Kinds of form submission
KindWhat happensWatch out for
Opens an email appThe visitor's own email app opens with a prepared message. Nothing is sent until they press send.It needs a configured email app. The website receives nothing itself. The page should say so.
Sends through a form serviceThe website passes the message to a service that emails it to you.Spam filtering, delivery failures and where the data is stored.
Saves to your own systemThe message is stored in a database or system and notifies you.You are now responsible for storing it securely and for keeping it for as long as needed.

None is wrong. What matters is that the confirmation message is true. Saying "Thanks, we have your message" when it only opened an email app, or when delivery failed, is how enquiries disappear. The shorter form is not magic. It simply asks for what the owner needs to reply and tells the truth about what happened. If you promise a reply time, make sure someone is there to keep it.

A delivery-testing checklist

  • Submit the form from a phone and a computer, using a different email address from yours.
  • Check that the message arrives, and how long it takes.
  • Check the spam folder of the receiving mailbox.
  • Submit with a field missing and confirm the error is clear and nothing is lost.
  • Disconnect from the internet and submit. Is the failure message honest?
  • Reply to the message. Does it go to the person who wrote it?
  • Repeat monthly, and after any change to your website or email.

When delivery problems need an expert

If tests sometimes pass and sometimes fail, the cause is usually email delivery or spam filtering, which is hard to diagnose without access to the settings. Help is also worthwhile if enquiries must feed a booking or customer system, or if you collect sensitive information. A clear brief helps anyone work out what the form must do, and the enquiry-to-booking workflow shows what should happen after the message arrives.
